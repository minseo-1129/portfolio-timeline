Multimodal Digital Phenotyping

Exploring Multimodal Signals for Children’s Mental Health Screening

A tablet-based assessment combined heart-rate variability, gaze, and voice to explore signals beyond self-report.

Period · 2024 Type · Digital Biomarkers · Child Mental Health · Multimodal Assessment Participants · 54 children aged 7–11
Research question

Can multimodal behavioural and physiological signals provide complementary evidence for children’s mental-health screening beyond self-report?

01 · PROBLEM

When self-report is not enough

Mental-health screening often relies heavily on self-report or caregiver report. For younger children, both sources can be incomplete.

Children may have difficulty recognising or verbalising internal experiences, while adults can only infer those experiences from what they observe.

Self-reportDirect access to the child’s account, but dependent on recognition, language, and willingness to report.
Observed / sensed dataPotentially complementary evidence, but still requiring careful interpretation and validation.
02 · COLLECTION

Three modalities through a tablet

The assessment combined three forms of digital biomarker collection rather than searching for one universal marker.

PhysiologyHeart-rate variabilityThe tablet camera supported remote photoplethysmography for HRV-related features.
AttentionEye trackingThe front-facing camera measured gaze during tasks with emotional facial stimuli.
ExpressionVoiceFree-speech and picture-description tasks extracted acoustic characteristics.
03 · SIGNALS

Different outcomes were associated with different signals

The strongest relationships were not distributed evenly across modalities.

DepressionHRV + gazeAssociated with physiological measures and time spent attending to sad facial expressions.
AnxietyMostly HRVMost consistently explained by heart-rate-variability features rather than gaze or voice.
StressPhysiological patternAssociated with HRV-related features, with some relationships shifting across grade groups.

Voice features showed no significant overall correlations with clinical scales in the full sample — the broader pattern suggests complementarity, not equal contribution from every modality.

04 · MODELS

Explained variance in symptom scores

Regression models provided a second view of the same pattern: the amount of variance explained differed substantially by outcome.

Explained variance · R²
Depression
30.2% LF + time viewing sad faces
Anxiety
42.9% LF/HF + heart rate + pNN50
Stress
16.4% pNN50

These are not diagnostic performance scores — they show where combinations of features may warrant further validation.

05 · DEVELOPMENT

Development is part of the measurement problem

Relationships between biomarkers were not necessarily stable across age groups.

Additional analyses separating lower and upper elementary grades found different patterns for some HRV and voice features. Biomarkers established in adults — or even older children — cannot automatically be assumed to carry the same meaning in younger children.

PhysiologyAutonomic regulation changes with development.
AttentionVisual attention and emotional processing are still developing.
ExpressionSpeech, language, and affective expression vary substantially across childhood.

54 children aged 7–11, no clinically diagnosed depression/anxiety groups recruited. The work is framed as exploratory and not suitable, by itself, for clinical diagnosis.

Design takeaway

Multimodal signals can complement self-report — but combining more signals does not make a system diagnostic.
