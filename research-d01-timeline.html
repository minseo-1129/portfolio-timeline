REDI

Designing from How Adolescents Already Record Their Lives

Before building a digital phenotype system, I studied the self-tracking practices adolescents had already invented for themselves.

Period · Nov 2022 – Mar 2023 Type · Formative Research · Diary Study · Adolescent Self-Tracking Participants · 34 adolescents aged 13–18
Research question

How do adolescents naturally record their lives when we do not prescribe a digital interface or rigid structure?

01 · FRAMING

Start with behaviour, not the app

Active digital phenotyping depends on people repeatedly entering information about their lives. If recording feels mainly like work performed for a research system, sustained participation becomes difficult.

Before designing an application, we began with adolescents’ existing self-recording behaviour rather than a predetermined digital interface.

System-first questionHow should adolescents enter the data we want?
Behaviour-first questionHow do adolescents naturally record their lives when we don’t prescribe the interface or structure?
02 · STUDY

A three-day physical diary study

Thirty-four adolescents aged 13–18 self-recorded their everyday lives using mailed physical sheets. The three-day period covered two weekdays and one weekend.

Semi-structured interviews then explored what participants chose to record, how they organised it, why they recorded, and what value they expected from a digital version.

03 · PURPOSE

They were often managing time, not collecting health data

Participants didn’t necessarily think of self-recording as “health tracking.” Study, sleep, leisure, exercise, and social activity appeared together because recording was often part of managing time.

Research lensCollect phenotype dataThe records can become repeated behavioural and emotional information for mental-health research.
Participant lensUnderstand daily lifeThe same records help adolescents see how time was spent and judge whether life felt balanced.

This reframed the future system: if the interface only expressed the researcher’s purpose, it could miss the reason adolescents were willing to record in the first place.

04 · TRANSLATION

Turning observed habits into design goals

The formative study produced four design directions that later shaped REDI.

Goal alignmentFamiliar planner and timetable representations aligned collection with adolescents’ own purpose for recording.
Reduced burdenClick-based input, familiar activity tags, and short EMA reduced the cost of repeated recording.
InterpretationVisual reports and deeper interpretation returned meaning instead of letting data disappear into a database.
Fit with routinesEMA timing and supportive phrasing were designed around moments adolescents could realistically respond.

The important translation is not paper diary → digital application. It's existing purpose and habit → interaction mechanism.

Design takeaway

Successful digital phenotyping starts with understanding why people already record their lives, then translating that purpose into the collection system.
