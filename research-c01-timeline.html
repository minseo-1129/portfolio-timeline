Community Care AI

Mapping What Conversational AI Can Actually Do in Community Care

A capability-centred review moves the conversation from AI labels to concrete functions, responsibilities, and care-system implications.

Period · Jun 2025 – Mar 2026 Type · Scoping Review · Conversational AI · Community Care Corpus · 29 included studies
Research question

What capabilities have conversational AI systems actually demonstrated in community-care settings?

01 · FRAMING

“AI assistant” is not a useful enough description

Labels such as “LLM chatbot”, “virtual assistant”, or “conversational agent” say surprisingly little about what a system actually contributes to a care pathway.

Community care is distributed across homes, neighbourhoods, frontline services, and professional teams. Practitioners need a vocabulary that describes concrete system functions rather than only underlying technology.

Model-centred questionShould this service use an LLM?
Capability-centred questionWhat should the system remember, assess, generate, or escalate — and who remains responsible?
02 · REVIEW

From 551 screened records to 29 studies

The review followed PRISMA-ScR guidance and searched biomedical and computing literature, supplemented with selected grey literature.

551records identified
541title/abstract screened
65full-text assessed
29studies included

Capabilities were coded at the level of specific functional actions. The final codebook was refined through independent coding, comparison, and consensus discussion.

03 · TAXONOMY

A 20-capability vocabulary

The resulting taxonomy organises 20 capabilities into five functional domains.

UnderstandIdentifyDetectClassifyRankAssess
TransformTranscribeTranslateAdapt
RememberStoreRetrieveRecall
GenerateConverseAnswerSummariseRenderWrite
ActNotifyEscalateScheduleOperate

The verbs are intentionally action-oriented — a team can reason about whether a system should recall past conversations or escalate a case more concretely than from a generic label like “AI assistant”.

04 · ROLE

Conversational AI often acted as infrastructure

Across the reviewed systems, conversational AI was commonly positioned as extending human care rather than replacing it.

AccessExtend reachAnswer questions, maintain contact, support users outside conventional encounters.
CoordinationMove informationStore, summarise, notify, and connect information across people and services.
OversightKeep humans in the loopEscalate uncertainty or risk, support frontline staff rather than assume autonomous care.
05 · RESPONSIBILITY

Every capability also creates a responsibility

A capability isn’t only a benefit — it changes what the surrounding care system must govern.

TensionEffect
Reliability ↔ flexibilityOpen-ended generation can feel natural while becoming harder to govern in sensitive interactions.
Memory ↔ privacyContinuity improves when systems remember; privacy and governance risk increase with persistence.
Access ↔ exclusionScalable support can widen reach while creating barriers for some users.
Automation ↔ labourMonitoring can scale, but someone still needs to interpret and respond.

The taxonomy is most useful when a capability is discussed together with the human responsibility, workflow, and safeguard around it.

Design takeaway

For conversational AI to be deployed responsibly in community care, we first need to understand what capabilities it actually provides — and match those capabilities to appropriate human roles and responsibilities.
