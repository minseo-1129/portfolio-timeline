When Agent Architecture Becomes Interaction Design

The same conversational intentions produced different forms of reflection when organised as one prompt versus multiple functional nodes.

Period · Oct 2025 – Jan 2026 Type · LLM Architecture · Human–AI Interaction · SDT · Motivational Interviewing Participants · 20 participants · 11 paired participants · 22 conversations
Research question

Does the way conversational responsibilities are organised change what kind of support the agent actually provides?

01 · VARIABLE

Architecture is invisible to users — but its consequences are not

Two agents can share the same interface, model, purpose, safety boundaries, and design principles yet create different interactions.

Writing “be empathetic” does not guarantee an interaction will feel empathetic. Designing around Self-Determination Theory does not guarantee that people will experience autonomy, competence, or relatedness. Internal architecture became a design variable rather than only an implementation detail.

02 · FRAMEWORKS

SDT described the experience; MI described the behaviour

Self-Determination TheoryWhat experience?Autonomy · meaningful choice and ownership. Competence · feeling capable of moving forward. Relatedness · feeling respected, connected, supported.
Motivational InterviewingWhat behaviour?Open questions · reflection · affirmation · collaboration · support for personal choice.

SDT provided the experiential target; MI gave a language for examining whether the agent’s dialogue actually enacted reflective and motivationally sensitive behaviour.

03 · ARCHITECTURES

Two ways of organising the same responsibilities

Single-promptIntegrated responsibilityReflection, goal management, activity recommendation, safety, tone, and interaction principles lived in one instruction space.
Multi-nodeSeparated responsibilityReflection, goal management, and activity recommendation were specialised into functional nodes connected through routing.
04 · EXPERIENCE

Users did not simply rate one architecture as better

Task-level METUX ratings were broadly comparable across the two architectures.

DimensionSingle-promptMulti-nodep
Autonomy5.635.55.635
Competence5.775.52.271
Relatedness4.454.38.983
Overall5.285.15.575
Single-promptMore open, emotionally attentive, companion-like; offered space to explore but could feel directionless.
Multi-nodeMore structured, practical, advisor-like; clearer progression but could move toward action before the person felt understood.

Similar need-support scores concealed different interactional pathways.

05 · REFLECTION

Reflection as process vs reflection as progress

Reflection as processStay with an experience → express it → interpret it → discover what matters.
Reflection as progressTurn understanding into direction → clarify options → make decisions → identify a next step.

Neither form is inherently superior — the design problem is deciding when someone needs space to remain with an experience and when they need help moving forward.

06 · BEHAVIOUR

MITI showed what the agent actually did

Behavioural annotation made the architectural difference visible in the dialogue itself.

Single-promptMore reflectionMore simple reflections (p = .001), higher total-reflection proportion (p = .005), higher reflection-to-question ratio (p = .009).
Multi-nodeMore information-givingHigher information-giving proportion (p = .004). Zero-reflection cases occurred only here: 3/11 and 4/11 conversations.

Prompt inspection tells us what the designer asked for. User ratings tell us what the person felt. Behavioural annotation adds a third layer: what the agent actually did.

Design takeaway

Agent architecture can support the same basic psychological needs through different interactional behaviours — and those differences may appear differently in user experience and observable agent behaviour.
